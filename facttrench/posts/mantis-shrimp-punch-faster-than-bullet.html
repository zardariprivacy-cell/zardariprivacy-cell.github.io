The Mantis Shrimp Punches at 50 MPH — And Hits Twice

2026-11-28 · 3 min read · By FactTrench

It is about the length of your hand, weighs less than a hamster, and it owns the fastest punch in the animal kingdom. The peacock mantis shrimp, Odontodactylus scyllarus, does not nibble its food — it smashes it. When its club-like arm fires, it moves at up to 23 metres per second, about 50 miles per hour, and accelerates at up to 10,000 times the force of gravity — roughly the same acceleration as a bullet leaving a gun barrel. The whole strike takes about 2.7 milliseconds, less time than a blink takes to start.

A spring, a latch and a saddle

No muscle can move that fast on its own, so the mantis shrimp cheats — with engineering. When it cocks its arm, a large muscle squeezes a saddle-shaped piece of shell that works like a spring, and a latch locks everything in place. When the latch lets go, the spring snaps open and hurls the club forward. Researchers calculated that a typical strike needs at least 470,000 watts of power per kilogram of muscle — orders of magnitude more than the fastest muscle known can produce. The spring does the work; the muscle only winds it up.

It hits twice

The club is only the first hit. It moves so fast that it tears the water apart, leaving a trail of vapour bubbles in its wake. A fraction of a millisecond later, those bubbles collapse — and the collapse hits like a second punch. In 2005, researchers measured two force peaks in every strike, about 0.4 milliseconds apart: the club itself lands with up to 1,501 newtons of force, thousands of times the shrimp's body weight, and the collapsing bubbles add up to 504 newtons more. Cavitation is so violent it can pit the steel of boat propellers — and it can flash with light and heat as the bubbles implode.

A club that refuses to break

You would expect a club that hammers shells thousands of times to shatter. It doesn't. The club is built from layered, helically wound chitin fibres coated in hard minerals — a structure so tough that materials scientists studied it to design better body armour. It smashes snail shells, crab claws and oyster shells open, and it is strong enough to crack the glass of a standard aquarium, which is why keepers house these animals behind thick acrylic.

Eyes that see a world we can't

The punch is not its only superpower. The mantis shrimp's stalked eyes can see up to 12 colour channels — humans manage three — plus ultraviolet light and circularly polarised light, a kind of light no other animal is known to detect. Not every mantis shrimp is a smasher, though: 'spearers' have sharp, barbed arms for impaling soft fish, while 'smashers' like the peacock carry the hammer. Either way, it is a shrimp that fights like a boxer and sees like an alien.

Quick Facts

  • Peacock mantis shrimp (Odontodactylus scyllarus): club speed up to 23 m/s (50 mph), acceleration ~10,000 g
  • Whole strike takes about 2.7 milliseconds; peak impact force up to 1,501 N
  • Cavitation bubbles collapse ~0.4 ms later, adding up to 504 N — a second hit
  • Spring-and-latch mechanism: the strike needs ~470,000 W per kg of muscle, far beyond what muscle alone can do
  • Its eyes see up to 12 colour channels (humans see 3), plus ultraviolet and circularly polarised light

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast is the mantis shrimp's punch?

The peacock mantis shrimp's club reaches up to 23 metres per second (about 50 mph) in about 2.7 milliseconds, accelerating at up to 10,000 times gravity — measured by high-speed cameras in a 2004 Nature study.

What is cavitation, and why does the shrimp hit twice?

The club moves so fast it tears the water into vapour bubbles. When those bubbles collapse a fraction of a millisecond later, the collapse releases a second burst of force — up to 504 newtons — on top of the club's own impact.

Can a mantis shrimp break aquarium glass?

Yes — a strike is strong enough to crack the glass of a standard aquarium, so keepers house peacock mantis shrimp behind thick acrylic instead.
