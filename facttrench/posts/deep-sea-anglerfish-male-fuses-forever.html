The Deep-Sea Anglerfish: Her Boyfriend Becomes Part of Her Body

2026-11-21 · 3 min read · By FactTrench

A mile below the surface, in water so dark that sunlight has never reached it, a female anglerfish hangs motionless in the black. Above her huge, fang-filled mouth dangles a small glowing orb — a fishing lure made of living light. Any curious fish that swims toward the glow swims straight into her jaws. But the lure is not only for hunting. It is also how she finds love — the strangest love story in the animal kingdom.

A boyfriend smaller than your thumbnail

The male anglerfish is a different animal entirely. He has no lure, no fangs, and no interest in hunting. He is tiny — roughly a tenth of the female's size — and his only job is to find her. He follows her scent through the dark water, and when he finally bumps into a female, he bites her. And he never lets go. His mouth fuses to her skin, their blood vessels join, and their tissues merge into one body. From that moment on, he lives off her bloodstream, slowly losing his eyes and most of his organs, until he is little more than a small bump on her side — a permanent, living part of his mate.

The only case of its kind

Scientists call this sexual parasitism, and in adult vertebrates it happens nowhere else on Earth. It is the only known natural example of parabiosis — two adult animals sharing one circulatory system — in any vertebrate. It sounds like a horror story, but in the deep sea it is a practical solution: down there, finding a mate in the vast dark is so unlikely that once you find one, evolution says never let go. One female pulled from a trawler was found carrying six males attached to her body.

A light she doesn't make herself

Even her famous glow is borrowed. The anglerfish cannot produce light on its own. The orb at the tip of her lure is filled with glowing bacteria that live there in partnership with the fish — she provides them a home, and they provide the light that draws in her dinner. With her stretchy jaw and expandable stomach, she can swallow prey twice her own size, a useful skill when meals in the deep sea can be weeks apart.

The best-known species, the humpback anglerfish, is only about 15 centimetres long — the females, that is. The males of one species measure just 6 to 7 millimetres as adults, once thought to be the smallest vertebrates on record. Most deep-sea anglerfish live between 200 and 3,000 metres down, and some have been found as deep as 6,000 metres. Not every species fuses permanently — in some, the male attaches only briefly — but wherever it happens, it remains the most extreme love story the ocean has to tell.

Quick Facts

  • Female humpback anglerfish: about 15 cm long; her mate is a fraction of that size
  • Males of one species are just 6.2–7.3 mm long as adults
  • A fused male shares the female's bloodstream — the only parabiosis in adult vertebrates
  • The lure's glow comes from symbiotic bacteria, not the fish itself
  • She can swallow prey twice her own size; one female was found with six males attached

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the male anglerfish fuse to the female?

In the vast, dark deep sea, finding a mate is so rare that once a male finds a female, he bites and fuses to her permanently, sharing her bloodstream. It guarantees he will be there when she is ready to reproduce.

Does the anglerfish make its own light?

No. The glow in its lure comes from bioluminescent bacteria living inside the lure organ. The fish provides them a home, and they provide the light.

How deep do anglerfish live?

Most deep-sea anglerfish live between about 200 and 3,000 metres down, and some species have been found as deep as 6,000 metres.
